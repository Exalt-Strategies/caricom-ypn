The first question anyone asks about the network is which side it is on. The
answer is all of them, and that is the whole point.

## The problem is shared before the politics is

Walk into almost any parliament in the Caribbean Community and you will find
the same thing: a handful of young members, scattered across the benches,
each the youngest person in the room by a decade or more. They were elected by
different parties on different platforms. What they hold in common is not an
ideology. It is a problem.

The problem is that the institutions they have joined were not built with them
in mind. Committee rosters, sitting hours, the unwritten seniority of who
speaks and when, all of it assumes a member who arrived a generation ago. A
young member can spend a whole first term simply learning to be heard.

## What a caucus can do that a party cannot

A party asks you to agree. A caucus only asks you to show up. That lower bar
turns out to be the network's greatest asset, because it lets a government
member and an opposition member sit in the same training session and share the
same drafting notes without either betraying their side.

When the reserved committee-seat rule passed in its first chamber, it passed
because members from three parties voted for it. None of them had to abandon
their platform. They simply agreed that whoever the young members turn out to
be, there should be a seat for them at the table where the work is done.

## The method, in one line

Get elected under whatever banner you believe in. Then organise with everyone
else your age around the handful of things you all need, regardless of banner.
Deliver those, and bring the next cohort through. The party is where you
disagree. The caucus is where you get the plumbing fixed.

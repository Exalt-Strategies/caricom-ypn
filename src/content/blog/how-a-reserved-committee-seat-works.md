The network's best-known win is a single paragraph added to a parliament's
standing orders. It is worth understanding exactly what it changes, because the
power is in how modest it is.

## What the rule says

In its passed form, the rule requires that every select committee offer at
least one place to a sitting member under forty, where such a member is
available and willing to serve. That is the entire mechanism.

It does not create new committees. It does not add members to parliament. It
does not reserve a seat by party. It simply says that when the places on a
committee are handed out, at least one of them must be offered to a young
member first.

## Why committees, and not the floor

Most of the real work of a parliament happens in committee, not in the debates
that make the news. Committees scrutinise budgets, question ministers line by
line, and shape bills before they are ever voted on. A member who is never on a
committee can speak often and change little.

By putting young members where the detailed work happens, the rule trades
visibility for influence. It is a quieter place to sit, and a far more useful
one.

## What it does not solve

The rule gets a young member into the room. It does not make them effective
once they are there. That is what the candidate schools and the mentor pairings
are for, and it is why the network treats the rule as a beginning rather than a
finish line.

## How to bring it to your chamber

Chapters that want to move the rule can request the drafting notes from the
regional council. They include the passed language, the arguments that worked,
and the amendments that did not, so the next chamber does not have to learn the
same lessons twice.
